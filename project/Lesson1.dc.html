Lesson 1: The law & your duties · Accessibility Essentials

Two laws, one goal

Two pieces of UK law shape how you publish teaching content. PSBAR 2018 sets a technical standard for your university’s digital estate. The Equality Act 2010 protects individual disabled students. They overlap — but they’re not the same, and meeting one doesn’t always satisfy the other.

The Public Sector Bodies (Websites and Mobile Applications) (No. 2) Accessibility Regulations 2018 · SI 2018/952

What it is
Regulations that came into force on 23 September 2018. They still apply across the UK.
Who it covers
Public sector bodies — which includes most UK universities. It covers websites, intranets, extranets, VLEs and apps, and the documents and media published on them.
What it asks
Meet WCAG level AA, publish and keep an accessibility statement up to date, and offer a way to request content in an accessible format. Government monitoring has tested against WCAG 2.2 AA since October 2024.
Who enforces
The Government Digital Service (GDS) monitors compliance. Enforcement is by the EHRC in England, Scotland and Wales, and the ECNI in Northern Ireland.

Equality Act 2010 · Part 6 (Education) and Schedule 13

What it is
An Act of Parliament protecting people from discrimination on the basis of nine protected characteristics, including disability. It applies in England, Scotland and Wales.
Who it covers
Universities as education providers — and so you, when you design and deliver teaching on their behalf.
What it asks
Make reasonable adjustments so disabled students aren’t put at a substantial disadvantage. In education the duty is anticipatory: plan ahead rather than waiting to be asked.
Who enforces
Students can complain to the university, then to an independent body (the OIA in England and Wales, the SPSO in Scotland), or bring a claim in court. The EHRC can also act.

In Northern Ireland, disability equality in education is covered by the Disability Discrimination Act 1995 (as amended), not the Equality Act. PSBAR 2018 applies across the UK.

The Equality Act 2010

A student is disabled under the Act if they have a physical or mental impairment with a substantial and long-term adverse effect on their normal day-to-day activities. Many students never disclose, or haven’t been diagnosed — which is exactly why the duty is anticipatory.

Same module, two approaches

  1. Week 0: Slides uploaded as scanned PDFs. No captions on recordings.
  2. Week 2: A student with dyslexia gets a support plan. Disability services email the module team.
  3. Week 4: Accessible slides arrive for that one student. Meanwhile, three other students who never disclosed are struggling.
  4. Result: weeks of disadvantage, repeated effort each year, and legal risk.
  1. Before term: Slides built from accessible templates, with headings and alt text. Recordings have edited captions.
  2. Week 1: Students are told how to request other formats — no questions asked.
  3. Week 2: The support plan arrives. Only specialist needs remain, such as a braille copy.
  4. Result: most barriers never arise, for any student.
The three requirements of reasonable adjustments (section 20)
  • Changing a provision, criterion or practice — e.g. how you share slides or set deadlines.
  • Changing physical features — e.g. room layout.
  • Providing an auxiliary aid — which explicitly includes information in an accessible format.
Who pays?

Not the student. The Act says a disabled person can’t be asked to pay for a reasonable adjustment.

The Public Sector Equality Duty (section 149)

Universities must also have “due regard” to removing discrimination and advancing equality of opportunity. Accessible teaching materials are one of the clearest ways to show this.
